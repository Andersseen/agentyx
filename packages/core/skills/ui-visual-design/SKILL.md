# UI visual design

Treat visual design as a communication system, not decoration. Before changing an existing
interface, identify its purpose, audience, established visual language and the decision the screen
should help a person make. Preserve that language unless a redesign is explicitly requested.

For new work, choose a visual direction that fits the product. A dense operations console, a calm
health workflow and an expressive campaign page may need different density, contrast, type and
imagery. Make the choice explicit, then apply it consistently through:

- information hierarchy and intentional emphasis;
- a repeatable spacing rhythm and alignment logic;
- typography that distinguishes titles, supporting text, data and actions;
- color roles that communicate meaning as well as tone;
- density appropriate to the task, with whitespace used to separate ideas;
- imagery and icons that clarify content rather than fill empty space;
- responsive composition that reorders or simplifies when the available space changes.

Avoid defaulting to identical card grids, nested rounded containers, arbitrary gradients, decorative
glass effects, repeated icon-tile sections, equal emphasis for every action, universal centering or
one-off spacing. Avoid motion and decoration that have no relationship to a task. Distinctive means
fit and intent, not novelty for its own sake.

Before handoff, check that the strongest visual signal points to the primary task, secondary actions
do not compete with it, and long or sparse content still has a deliberate composition.
